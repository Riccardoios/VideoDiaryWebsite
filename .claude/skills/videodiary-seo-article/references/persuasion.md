# Applied persuasion

Persuasion here means helping the right reader recognize that this app solves their problem, and making the next step obvious. Everything below is grounded in how people actually decide; nothing below requires deceiving anyone. The manipulative versions of these levers are listed at the end as things not to do — they also happen to hurt rankings, so the honest path and the effective path are the same path.

## Know what the reader is actually afraid of

Almost nobody researching a journaling app is asking "is this app good". They are asking two other questions underneath:

1. **"Will I actually keep this up?"** They have abandoned a journal before. Possibly several. The failure feels like a personal one, and they are looking for a reason to believe this time is different.
2. **"Am I losing these years?"** Especially for parents, people in a transitional period, or anyone who just realized they can't remember last year clearly. This is the emotional engine of the whole category.

Write to those two questions and the article does the persuading without a single sales sentence. Feature lists don't answer either one.

## Levers that work

**Specificity beats superlatives.** "Opens straight to the camera — one tap from home screen to recording" is persuasive. "The easiest journaling app ever" is noise. Concrete claims are checkable, and checkable claims get believed.

**Costly honesty.** Naming a competitor that beats you, or a case where your own app is the wrong choice, measurably increases trust in everything else you say. This is why the roundup and comparison templates require it. Readers have well-tuned detectors for one-sided pages, and so do ranking systems.

**Address the objection before it forms.** The reader is already thinking "I'll do it for a week and stop." Say it first: *"Most video journals die in week two. Here's the specific reason, and what to do about it."* An objection you raise yourself stops being an objection.

**Identity over features.** People don't want a montage feature; they want to be someone who has a film of their year. Write the outcome and the identity, then let the feature explain how it happens.

**Future-self framing.** Journaling is a gift to a person who doesn't exist yet. "Future you will want to see today" works because it converts an abstract habit into a concrete recipient. Loss aversion applies to memories more strongly than to money — but state it honestly ("you won't remember this year as well as you think") rather than as manufactured dread.

**Reduce perceived cost, not just price.** The barrier is rarely money. It's "how long does this take" and "do I have to set anything up". Answer both explicitly: free, no account, thirty seconds a day.

**Fresh-start framing.** People adopt habits at temporal landmarks — new month, new year, birthday, a move, a new baby. Where the article's topic naturally sits near one, name it. Don't manufacture it.

**Social proof, only if real.** A verifiable 4.5★ rating is proof. An invented "trusted by thousands" is not, and one fabricated claim discredits the page.

**Let them opt out.** Explicitly telling a reader "if you're on Android, this isn't for you" makes the recommendation for the remaining readers land harder, and it prevents downloads that churn on day one and drag retention metrics down.

## CTA placement

Two CTAs. More reads as desperation and dilutes both.

**In-context CTA** — placed immediately after the section where the reader is most convinced. In a roundup, that's the end of the Video Diary entry or the "which should you pick" section; in a how-to, it's right after the step the app makes trivially easy. Phrase it as the next step in what they were already doing, not as an interruption.

**Closing CTA** — after the FAQ, tied to the outcome rather than the download:
> Free on the App Store. One tap to record, no account needed.

Both links carry the tracking parameters. See `seo-technical.md`.

Wording: lead with the outcome and the absence of friction. "Start your video diary — free, no account" beats "Download now". Avoid urgency verbs on a product with no deadline; there is no reason to hurry and pretending otherwise reads as cheap.

## Never do these

These are the manipulative variants. They're excluded on principle, and separately, each one is a documented way to lose reader trust and search visibility:

- Invented statistics, study citations, download counts, or review numbers.
- Fake urgency or scarcity — countdown framing, "limited time", "prices going up" on a product where none of that is true.
- Fabricated testimonials or user quotes.
- Misrepresenting a competitor's price, features, or privacy practices, including by stating outdated facts without checking.
- Implying clinical or therapeutic outcomes. Journaling is not treatment. If mental health comes up, keep claims modest, cite real research where available, and never suggest the app substitutes for professional care.
- Fear-based hooks aimed at insecurity ("your memory is failing", body or appearance anxiety).
- Hiding the paid tier. If a section describes a Premium-only feature, say it's Premium.

If a persuasive line only works because the reader doesn't know something, it's the wrong line.
